## Summary
Stock charts are visual records of how a share's price has moved over time. Learning to read them helps you see patterns, understand momentum, and make more informed decisions — even if you're not a professional trader.

## Key Concepts
- **Candlestick**: A common chart element that shows a stock's opening price, closing price, and the highest and lowest prices within a given time period.
- **Volume**: The number of shares traded during a period. High volume often confirms the strength of a price move.
- **Moving average (MA)**: The average price of a stock over a set number of days (e.g., 50-day or 200-day), smoothed into a line to filter out short-term noise.
- **Support and resistance**: Price levels where a stock has historically tended to stop falling (support) or stop rising (resistance).
- **Trend**: The general direction of a stock's price over time — upward (bullish), downward (bearish), or sideways.

## Explanation
A stock chart is essentially a timeline of price. The horizontal axis shows time (minutes, days, months — depending on how you set it), and the vertical axis shows price. At a glance, you can see whether a stock has been rising, falling, or moving sideways. Most investment platforms, including those at [Oslo Børs](https://www.oslobors.no/) and trading apps like Nordnet, provide interactive charts for every listed stock.

The most widely used chart style is the **candlestick chart**, developed in Japan centuries ago. Each "candle" covers a specific time period — say, one trading day. The body of the candle shows the range between the opening and closing price. If the stock closed *higher* than it opened, the candle is typically green (or white). If it closed *lower*, it's red (or black). The thin lines above and below the body — called *wicks* or *shadows* — show the highest and lowest prices hit during that period. At a glance, a chart full of green candles with short wicks tells a different story than one full of long red candles.

Moving averages smooth out the noise. The 50-day moving average, for example, takes the closing prices of the last 50 trading days and plots their average as a line. When the actual price is well above the moving average, the stock may be considered in a strong uptrend. When it falls below, momentum may be weakening. Traders watch when shorter-term averages cross longer-term ones — a "golden cross" (50-day crosses above 200-day) is often seen as bullish, while a "death cross" (50-day drops below 200-day) signals potential weakness. As [Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/m/movingaverage.asp) notes, moving averages are *lagging* indicators — they tell you what has already happened, not what will happen next.

Support and resistance levels are price points where buying or selling pressure has historically clustered. If a stock keeps bouncing back up every time it hits 150 NOK, that's a support level. If it keeps failing to break through 200 NOK, that's resistance. These levels don't always hold — they break — but they're useful reference points.

Volume is the often-overlooked companion to price. A big price jump on very low volume can be a fragile move; a price rise on surging volume suggests broader conviction behind the movement.

## Example
You pull up a 6-month candlestick chart for a mid-cap Norwegian company on [Oslo Børs](https://www.oslobors.no/). You notice the stock has been making higher highs and higher lows — a classic uptrend. The 50-day moving average sits comfortably below the current price, confirming the upward momentum. There's a clear resistance level at 320 NOK where the stock has stalled twice before. Volume has been picking up on up days and shrinking on down days, which is generally a positive sign. You don't know the future — but you now have a much clearer picture of the stock's recent behaviour than you would from just a single price quote.
