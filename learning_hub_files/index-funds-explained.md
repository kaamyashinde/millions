## Summary
An index fund is an investment that automatically tracks a market index — like the 500 largest US companies or the entire Norwegian stock market — giving you instant, broad diversification at low cost.

## Key Concepts
- **Index**: A list of stocks used to represent a market or segment of it. The S&P 500 tracks 500 large US companies; the Oslo Børs Benchmark Index (OSEBX) tracks the most traded Norwegian stocks.
- **Index fund**: A fund that holds all (or most) of the stocks in its target index, so its performance mirrors the index.
- **Passive investing**: An investment approach that aims to match the market rather than beat it. Index funds are the main tool for passive investing.
- **Expense ratio**: The annual fee charged by the fund, expressed as a percentage of your investment. Index funds typically charge 0.05%–0.20%, far below most actively managed funds.
- **ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund)**: A type of index fund that trades on a stock exchange like a regular stock, so you can buy and sell it throughout the trading day.

## Explanation
Every year, financial analysts and fund managers try to pick stocks that will outperform the market. Most fail. Research consistently shows that the majority of actively managed funds do worse than simply buying every stock in the market and holding on. This observation, popularised by economist John Bogle — who founded Vanguard — led to the creation of index funds in the 1970s.

The logic is straightforward: instead of trying to pick winning stocks, just buy them all. When you invest in an S&P 500 index fund, your money is spread across 500 companies in proportion to their size. Apple might be 7% of the fund, a smaller company might be 0.1%. If the overall market rises 10%, your fund rises roughly 10% too — minus a tiny fee.

The fee difference matters enormously over time. An actively managed fund might charge 1–2% per year in fees. An index fund might charge 0.07%. Thanks to compounding, that difference in cost can cost you tens of thousands of NOK over a 30-year investment horizon, even if the active fund performs identically to the index.

For Norwegian investors, index funds covering global markets (such as those tracking the MSCI World index) are popular through platforms like [Nordnet](https://www.nordnet.no/magasinet/investering/indeksfond/) and DNB. These give exposure to hundreds of companies across dozens of countries in a single, inexpensive product. As [investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/mutual-funds-and-exchange-traded-funds-etfs) notes, funds with lower costs give investors a better chance of achieving market-rate returns, which is why index funds are often the recommended starting point for beginners.

Index funds aren't perfect — they'll fall when the market falls, they can't avoid market-wide crashes, and they give you no chance of beating the market. But for most long-term investors, matching the market at low cost, automatically diversified, is a solid strategy.

## Example
You put 1,000 NOK per month into a global index fund with an expense ratio of 0.10%, tracking the MSCI World index. You don't need to pick stocks, follow company news, or rebalance your portfolio manually — the fund handles all of that. Over 30 years, assuming 7% average annual growth, you'd have contributed 360,000 NOK and your portfolio would be worth approximately 1.2 million NOK. The same strategy with a fund charging 1.5% in annual fees would leave you with roughly 860,000 NOK — around 340,000 NOK less, just from the fee difference.
