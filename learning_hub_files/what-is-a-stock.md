## Summary
A stock is a small piece of ownership in a company. When you buy a stock, you become a part-owner of that business — and share in its successes and failures.

## Key Concepts
- **Share**: A single unit of ownership in a company. A company divides itself into millions of these units when it goes public.
- **Stock market**: A marketplace — like Oslo Børs or the New York Stock Exchange — where shares are bought and sold.
- **Equity**: Another word for ownership. Owning stock means you hold *equity* in a company.
- **Dividend**: Some companies pay shareholders a portion of their profits on a regular basis; this payment is called a dividend.
- **IPO (Initial Public Offering)**: The moment a private company first sells shares to the public, allowing anyone to become an owner.

## Explanation
Imagine a friend starts a bakery and needs money to buy equipment. Instead of taking a bank loan, she splits the bakery into 100 equal "shares" and sells 40 of them to friends and family to raise cash. If you buy 10 shares, you own 10% of the bakery. That is essentially what a stock is — ownership sliced up into small, tradeable pieces.

Real companies work the same way, just on a much larger scale. When a company wants to raise money from the general public, it goes through an IPO and lists its shares on a stock exchange. After that, anyone — from a large pension fund to a student with a small savings account — can buy and sell those shares through a broker or an investment app.

As a shareholder, your financial outcome is tied to the company's. If the company grows and earns more money, the value of your shares usually rises. If the business struggles, your shares can fall in value. Some companies also reward shareholders by paying dividends — regular cash payments out of the company's profits.

Stocks are one of the most common ways individuals build wealth over time. Historically, the stock market has delivered stronger long-term returns than savings accounts or bonds, though it also comes with more short-term ups and downs. [Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/s/stock.asp) describes this clearly: stocks sit higher on the risk-return ladder than most other asset classes, which is why they tend to reward patient, long-term investors.

It is worth knowing that owning stock does *not* mean you run the company or get to decide how it operates day-to-day. That is the job of management. What you do get is a claim on a share of the company's assets and earnings — and the right to vote on major company decisions at annual shareholder meetings.

## Example
Suppose Equinor (a Norwegian energy company listed on [Oslo Børs](https://www.oslobors.no/)) has 3 billion shares outstanding, and each share costs 300 NOK. The total market value of the company — called its *market capitalisation* — is then 900 billion NOK. If you buy 10 shares for 3,000 NOK, you own a tiny fraction of that company. If Equinor's share price rises to 330 NOK, your 10 shares are now worth 3,300 NOK — a 10% gain. If the price drops to 270 NOK, you're sitting on a 10% loss on paper.
