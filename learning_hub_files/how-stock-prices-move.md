## Summary
Stock prices change constantly because buyers and sellers are always negotiating what a share is worth. Prices are driven by a mix of company performance, economic conditions, and plain old human emotion.

## Key Concepts
- **Supply and demand**: When more people want to buy a stock than sell it, the price rises; when more want to sell, it falls.
- **Earnings**: A company's profits are one of the strongest drivers of its share price. Better-than-expected profits often push the price up.
- **Market sentiment**: The overall mood of investors — optimistic or pessimistic — can move prices even without any change in a company's fundamentals.
- **Volatility**: How much a stock's price swings up and down over a period of time.
- **Market capitalisation (market cap)**: The total value of all a company's shares combined, calculated as share price × number of shares.

## Explanation
At its core, a stock price is just what someone is willing to pay for a share at any given moment. Millions of buyers and sellers place orders on exchanges like [Oslo Børs](https://www.oslobors.no/) or the New York Stock Exchange every day, and the price settles wherever supply meets demand.

Several things influence that supply and demand. Company performance is the biggest one. Every quarter, public companies report their earnings — how much money they made. If the results beat what analysts were expecting, investors get excited and buy more shares, pushing the price up. If earnings disappoint, some investors sell, and the price drops. This is why you often see large price swings right after a company's earnings announcement.

Broader economic conditions also matter. When interest rates rise, borrowing becomes more expensive for companies, which can hurt profits. Investors may then move money out of stocks and into safer assets like bonds, pulling stock prices down. Similarly, news about inflation, unemployment, or GDP growth can shift the mood of the entire market in hours.

Then there is sentiment — the psychological side. Fear and greed play a real role. During a financial crisis, even healthy companies can see their shares plummet because investors panic and sell everything. During a bull market (a period of rising prices), investors may pay prices that seem high relative to current earnings because they expect strong future growth. As [Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/articles/basics/04/100804.asp) notes, a stock's price reflects not just what a company *is* worth today, but what investors *believe* it will be worth in the future.

Individual company news — a new product launch, a CEO resignation, a merger, a lawsuit — can also send a share price sharply up or down in minutes. This is why keeping up with company news matters if you own individual stocks.

## Example
Say a Norwegian tech company reports quarterly earnings of 50 million NOK — well above the 35 million NOK analysts had forecast. Investors are impressed, demand for the shares spikes, and the price jumps 8% in a single day. A week later, the Norwegian central bank announces a surprise interest rate hike. Investors worry this will slow the economy, so they sell riskier assets including tech stocks. The same share gives back half its gains. No new company news came out — just a change in the economic environment.
