## Summary
Compound interest means earning returns not just on your original investment, but also on the returns you've already made. Over time, this creates a snowball effect that can turn small, regular savings into significant wealth.

## Key Concepts
- **Simple interest**: Interest calculated only on your original amount. 5% on 1,000 always gives you 50.
- **Compound interest**: Interest calculated on your growing balance — including previously earned interest. Your earnings start earning their own earnings.
- **Principal**: The original amount of money you invest or deposit.
- **Compounding frequency**: How often interest is added to your balance — daily, monthly, or annually. More frequent compounding means slightly faster growth.
- **Time horizon**: The single most important factor in compounding. The longer your money is invested, the more powerful the effect.

## Explanation
Imagine a snowball rolling down a long hill. It starts small, but as it rolls it picks up more snow, grows bigger, and picks up even more snow with each rotation. Compound interest works the same way. The longer it rolls, the bigger it gets — and the speed of growth accelerates over time.

Here is the simple mechanics: if you invest 10,000 NOK and earn 7% in the first year, you end up with 10,700 NOK. In year two, you earn 7% on 10,700 — not just on the original 10,000. That gives you 11,449 NOK. Each year, the base you're earning on gets larger. After 30 years at 7% annual growth, that original 10,000 NOK grows to over 76,000 NOK — without adding a single extra krone.

The formula behind this is: **A = P × (1 + r)^t**, where A is the final amount, P is the principal, r is the annual interest rate, and t is the number of years. The exponent — the *t* — is what makes compounding feel almost magical over long periods. As [Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org/economics-finance-domain/core-finance/interest-tutorial/compound-interest-tutorial/a/compound-interest-basics) explains, the effect is modest in the early years but becomes dramatic over decades.

Starting early matters enormously. An investor who puts in 5,000 NOK a year from age 20 to 30 (10 years, then stops) will likely end up with more money at retirement than someone who starts at 30 and contributes 5,000 NOK every year until they retire at 65 — simply because the early investor gave their money more time to compound. This is sometimes called the advantage of *time in the market*.

It also works in reverse. Credit card debt and consumer loans compound against you. The same maths that builds wealth when you invest can dig you deeper into debt if you're borrowing at high interest rates. This is why paying off high-interest debt is often the smartest financial move before you start investing.

## Example
You invest 5,000 NOK at age 22 and never add another krone. It earns an average of 7% per year (roughly the long-run historical average for a global stock index). By age 62 — 40 years later — it has grown to approximately 74,872 NOK. Now compare: your friend waits until age 32 to make the same 5,000 NOK investment at the same 7% rate. By age 62 that's only 30 years of compounding, and the ending balance is about 38,061 NOK. A 10-year head start nearly doubled the final outcome, even though the initial investment was identical.
