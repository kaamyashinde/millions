## Summary
Stocks and bonds are the two most common building blocks of an investment portfolio. Stocks give you ownership in a company; bonds are loans you make to a company or government in exchange for regular interest payments.

## Key Concepts
- **Stock (equity)**: A share of ownership in a company. Your return depends on how well the company performs.
- **Bond (debt)**: A loan you give to a company or government. They promise to pay you back with interest over a set period.
- **Coupon**: The regular interest payment a bond issuer pays you, usually once or twice a year.
- **Maturity date**: The date on which the bond issuer repays the original amount you lent (called the *principal*).
- **Risk-return trade-off**: Stocks typically offer higher long-term returns than bonds, but with more volatility. Bonds offer more stability but usually less growth.

## Explanation
When a company or government needs money, it has two main options: sell ownership stakes (stocks) or borrow money (bonds). As an investor, you get to choose which side of that equation you're on.

Buying a stock makes you a part-owner. You benefit when the company grows and suffers when it struggles. There is no guaranteed return — your income comes from price appreciation (the stock going up in value) and potentially from dividends. Over long periods, stocks have historically outperformed most other asset types. According to [investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/bonds), U.S. stocks have returned an average of around 10% per year historically, compared with around 4–6% for bonds — though past returns don't guarantee future ones.

Buying a bond is more like lending money than owning something. You agree to lend a company or government a fixed amount for a set term — say, 5 years — and in return they pay you interest (the coupon) at regular intervals, then return your principal at the maturity date. Government bonds issued by stable countries (like Norwegian government bonds, or *statsobligasjoner*) are considered very low risk because the government is unlikely to default. Corporate bonds carry more risk because companies can go bankrupt.

The key difference comes down to priority. If a company goes bankrupt, bondholders are paid back before stockholders. Stockholders only get whatever is left — which can be nothing. This is a big reason why bonds are considered safer.

Most well-rounded long-term portfolios hold a mix of both. Younger investors often hold more stocks (more time to ride out losses), while those closer to retirement typically shift toward more bonds (more stability, less downside). This is a personal decision that depends on your time horizon and risk tolerance.

## Example
You invest 20,000 NOK. You put 10,000 in a Norwegian government bond paying 3.5% annual interest over 5 years. Each year you receive 350 NOK in interest, and at the end of 5 years you get your 10,000 back — predictable and steady. You put the other 10,000 in a stock. After 5 years, it could be worth 18,000 NOK (an 80% gain) — or it could have dropped to 6,000 NOK. The bond was less exciting, but you always knew what you were getting. The stock had more upside and more downside.
