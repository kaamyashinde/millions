## Summary
Investment risk is the possibility that your investment loses value — or doesn't grow as much as you hoped. Understanding it helps you make smarter decisions about where to put your money.

## Key Concepts
- **Risk vs. return**: Higher potential returns almost always come with higher risk. There is no free lunch in investing.
- **Volatility**: How much an asset's price bounces around. A highly volatile investment can gain or lose a lot of value in a short time.
- **Market risk**: The risk that the entire market falls, dragging your investment down with it regardless of how good the company is.
- **Diversification**: Spreading your money across different investments to reduce the impact of any single one performing badly. (Covered in more depth in its own topic.)
- **Time horizon**: How long you plan to stay invested. A longer time horizon generally lets you ride out short-term losses.

## Explanation
Risk sounds scary, but it's simply the uncertainty around what an investment will return. Every investment carries some level of it — even a savings account carries the risk that inflation (the rising cost of living) erodes the real value of your money over time.

The most talked-about risk is market risk: the chance that prices fall. Stock markets go through periods of decline — sometimes sharp and sudden, sometimes long and grinding. A company can also have problems specific to itself: a product fails, a competitor takes its market share, or management makes a bad decision. This is called *company-specific risk* (also known as *unsystematic risk*), and it is the type of risk you can reduce by not putting all your money into one company.

Then there is *liquidity risk* — the risk that you cannot sell your investment when you need cash, or that you can only sell it at a much lower price than expected. Stocks of large companies are generally very easy to sell. Investments like real estate or small private companies are much harder to exit quickly.

Volatility is often used as a stand-in for risk. According to [Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/r/risk.asp), standard deviation — a measure of how much returns vary around an average — is one of the most common ways analysts quantify investment risk. A stock that swings 20% up or down in a year is riskier than one that moves 4%.

The key insight every investor eventually internalises is that risk and return are two sides of the same coin. Government bonds (loans to governments) are generally considered very safe, but they offer modest returns. Small, fast-growing companies can offer large returns — but they can also go to zero. Understanding your own *risk tolerance* (how much loss you can stomach emotionally and financially) is one of the first steps in building a sensible investment strategy.

## Example
You have 10,000 NOK to invest. Option A is a government bond paying a guaranteed 3% per year. Option B is a single stock in a small tech startup that could double in value — or lose 80% of its value. Option A gives you certainty but modest growth: your 10,000 grows to roughly 10,300 after a year. Option B might turn your 10,000 into 20,000, but it might also fall to 2,000. Which is right for you depends on your timeline, your financial cushion, and how you'd feel waking up to that 80% loss.
