## Summary
Paper trading means simulating stock market trades with fake money, so you can learn how investing works without putting any real money at risk. It's a practical way for beginners to build confidence before investing for real.

## Key Concepts
- **Paper trading**: Tracking hypothetical trades on paper (or in a simulator) as if they were real, without using actual money.
- **Trading simulator**: An app or platform that replicates the real market with virtual funds, allowing you to buy and sell as if it were real.
- **Portfolio tracking**: Recording and monitoring the performance of your simulated investments over time.
- **Execution price**: The price at which a trade actually goes through. In real markets, this can differ slightly from the price you see — something paper trading helps you experience without financial consequence.
- **Emotional discipline**: One thing paper trading *cannot* fully replicate. The emotional experience of real money being at stake changes how people make decisions.

## Explanation
The best way to learn something is to do it — but with investing, the cost of mistakes can be real. Paper trading bridges that gap. You pick stocks, ETFs, or other assets, "buy" them at today's market prices using virtual money, and track how your portfolio performs over days, weeks, or months. No real money changes hands.

The name comes from the old practice of writing your hypothetical trades in a notebook — recording the date, the stock, the price, and the number of shares. Today, most people use online simulators that pull live market data automatically, making the experience much closer to real trading.

Paper trading is genuinely useful for learning the mechanics: how to place different types of orders (market orders, limit orders), what spreads and transaction costs look like, how diversification affects a portfolio in practice, and how quickly a "sure thing" can turn against you. You might think you understand risk intellectually, but watching a simulated 10% portfolio loss play out in real time — even with fake money — teaches something that reading about it cannot.

That said, paper trading has well-known limits. Because there's no real money on the line, you don't feel the same fear when your portfolio drops, or the same greed when it rises. [Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/p/papertrade.asp) flags this directly: many paper traders take risks they would never take with real money, which means their simulated results can look much better than their eventual real-money performance. The lesson is to treat your paper portfolio as seriously as possible — set a realistic starting amount, avoid taking wild bets you'd never take with real savings, and keep detailed notes on *why* you made each trade.

A few platforms that offer free paper trading or simulation features include Investopedia's own Stock Simulator, and several Norwegian investment apps that offer demo accounts. Some stock games run as competitions — for example, school or university investing contests — which add a social element and a bit of competitive pressure that makes the practice more realistic.

## Example
You open an account on Investopedia's free Stock Simulator with a virtual balance of 100,000 NOK (or USD equivalent). You decide to "buy" shares in a global index fund and two individual Norwegian companies you've been following. Over 3 months, the index fund grows steadily while one company jumps 15% after a strong earnings report and the other falls 12% after a product recall. You track your decisions in a journal: why you bought, what you expected, and what actually happened. When you eventually invest real money, you've already lived through a market cycle once — without it costing you anything.
