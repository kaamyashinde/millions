## Summary
Diversification means spreading your investments across different assets so that a loss in one doesn't wipe out your whole portfolio. It is one of the most practical tools in investing — and one of the few ways to reduce risk without necessarily reducing expected returns.

## Key Concepts
- **Correlation**: A measure of how closely two assets move together. Investments that are *uncorrelated* (move independently) provide stronger diversification than those that move in lockstep.
- **Asset class**: A broad category of investment, such as stocks, bonds, real estate, or cash. Each tends to behave differently under various economic conditions.
- **Concentration risk**: The danger of having too much money in a single stock, sector, or geography.
- **Systematic vs. unsystematic risk**: Systematic risk affects the entire market (e.g., a global recession). Unsystematic risk is specific to one company or industry. Diversification can eliminate unsystematic risk but *not* systematic risk.
- **Portfolio**: The full collection of investments you hold across all accounts.

## Explanation
The old saying "don't put all your eggs in one basket" is exactly what diversification is about. If you invest all your savings in one company's stock and that company collapses, you lose everything. If you spread that money across 30 different companies in different industries, the failure of one hurts you only a little.

But diversification goes further than just owning multiple stocks. True diversification means holding assets that don't all fall at the same time. If you own 20 Norwegian oil company stocks, you might think you're diversified — but they'll all tend to move together when oil prices drop. That is concentration risk disguised as diversification. What you want are assets with low *correlation*: when one zigs, another zags.

This is why mixing asset classes matters. Stocks and government bonds, for example, have historically had relatively low correlation. During stock market crashes, investors often flee to the safety of government bonds, which pushes bond prices *up* — partially offsetting stock losses. Adding international stocks to a Norwegian-only portfolio also helps, because different economies don't always boom and bust in sync with each other.

According to [Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/d/diversification.asp), research in modern portfolio theory (the academic framework behind diversification) suggests that most of the benefit of diversification is captured with around 20–30 well-chosen, uncorrelated stocks. Beyond that, each additional stock adds less and less extra protection. Index funds — which hold hundreds or thousands of stocks automatically — are an efficient way to achieve broad diversification without having to pick individual companies.

The limit of diversification is worth understanding: it can only remove unsystematic (company-specific) risk. It cannot protect you from a market-wide crash — that is systematic risk, and it hits everyone. Holding cash, bonds, and other non-stock assets helps moderate this exposure, but cannot eliminate it entirely.

## Example
Investor A puts 100,000 NOK entirely into one Norwegian shipping company. Investor B splits 100,000 NOK equally across a Norwegian shipping company, a Swedish technology firm, a German consumer goods company, a global bond index fund, and a gold ETF. If a global shipping crisis hits and shipping stocks fall 40%, Investor A loses 40,000 NOK. Investor B loses around 8,000 NOK on their shipping position — but the tech, consumer goods, bonds, and gold may be flat or even up, resulting in a much smaller total loss.
